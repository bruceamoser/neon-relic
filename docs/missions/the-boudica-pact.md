# The Boudica Pact

## The call

A Covenant Wayfinder monitoring station detected an anomalous Corruption signature in rural Staffordshire. The signature pattern is consistent with a breached supernatural binding, ancient, Roman-era in origin. The epicenter is a farm. The farmer, one Arthur Dunn, is missing.

## If the team does nothing

Arthur Dunn’s plough has lifted the keystone. First the crops grey around his farm, then birds fall silent for miles. Within a week the ground breathes and the Red Woman speaks. The Ministry of Agriculture will quarantine the farm when the blight reaches the neighboring fields. At the Plough and Furrow, the regulars remember passing a warm coin from hand to hand.

## Behind the incident

After Boudica's defeat in 61 CE, her dying curse was adopted by something older than the Celts. A presence in the earth that had no name, no form, no voice until she gave it one. It took her rage and made it eternal. The Romans called it "the Red Woman." It manifested as a creeping blight: crops withered, water soured, livestock starved in the womb. The Romans tried everything. In 78 CE, Agricola authorized a pact. Gold was buried at the blight's epicenter. As long as gold remained, the entity slept. The transaction was recorded in a ledger, dry, bureaucratic Latin, filed as an "exceptional expense." One copy went to Rome and was eventually archived in the Vatican. The Anglo-Saxons reinforced the binding in the 7th century with the Staffordshire Hoard. A Mercian monk named Eadwine recorded the reinforcement, between complaints about the food and his abbot.

In 1984, archaeologists excavated most of the Staffordshire Hoard. They missed one coin. The keystone, deeper than their radar reached. In 1987, farmer Arthur Dunn's plough struck it. He showed it around the pub, vague about what he'd found. "Something interesting." He left for a fishing trip to Scotland. He has not returned. His cottage is locked. His post is piling up. Within 72 hours of the coin's removal, the blight began returning, locusts navigating to the farm, water turning briny, a spreading silence.

The coin does not "activate" in the traditional sense. Its effect is passive and location-dependent. When buried in the earth at the binding locus (a specific point on Dunn's farm), the coin sustains a pact that keeps the bound entity dormant. When removed, the binding weakens. The coin itself is inert above ground. It is merely gold. The power is in the pact, not the object. The coin is a key. The lock is the earth. The door is the binding. Any gold from the original binding will work. The pact specifies "gold, measured by weight." It does not specify a quantity. The Romans were precise about loopholes. A single coin is sufficient.

The artifact (the keystone coin) is NOT what the agents recover. The coin was removed by the farmer, who is missing. The agents must acquire a REPLACEMENT coin from the Birmingham Museum to re-bury at the binding site. The museum coins are behind glass, in vaults, legally protected. The climax is a museum infiltration followed by a ritual re-burial.

## People

**The Townsfolk (The Plough and Furrow Regulars).** Composite: Conflicting Witnesses. Help Arthur, they're genuinely worried. Win the pub bet about what he found, they're also bored. The two motivations coexist comfortably, as they do in village life.

**Dr. Helen Price.** Curator of Anglo-Saxon Collections: Birmingham Museum. Protect the hoard. Complete her research. Find Unrecovered Item #7, her professional white whale. She is not an enemy. She is a scholar who is about to have her worldview adjusted.

**Mr. Samuel Okonkwo.** Night Security Guard: Birmingham Museum. Keep his job. Keep people safe. Understand what's happening in his museum. He is not a coward. He is a careful man in a world that punishes inconvenient observations.

**Father Matteo.** Archivist: Vatican Apostolic Archive. Preserve the archive. Serve the truth, even when the truth is uncomfortable. Help the agents without violating his oath. He will bend rules but not break them. He will expedite. He will translate. He will provide context. He will not forge authorization.

**Dr. Margaret Rhys.** Retired Archaeologist: Excavated the Staffordshire Hoard. Help the agents. See the hoard understood (truly understood) before she dies. She's 71. She has time, but not unlimited time. She wants her work to mean something. The anomalous coin was the most significant thing she ever found, and she let it go. She doesn't want to let it go again.

**Arthur Dunn (The Absent Farmer).** Accidental Catalyst: Missing. Go home. He can't. The coin won't let him, not until the binding is either restored or broken. He doesn't understand any of this. He's a farmer who found something odd in his field and now his life has been stolen by a force he can't name. He wants to sleep. He wants to go back to before he found the coin. He knows neither is possible.

**Mr. Philip Croft.** Ministry of Agriculture Investigator. Contain the agricultural crisis. Protect British farming. File a report that makes sense. He will accept explanations that don't make sense if they come with evidence. He is not an enemy. He is a civil servant who has run out of rational answers.

**The Entity (The Red Woman / Boudica's Curse).** Bound Presence: Antagonist as Condition, Not Character. Be. Take back. The entity doesn't want to destroy. It wants to occupy . It wants the land returned to a state before human agriculture, before human settlement, before human presence. It is not evil. It is incompatible. It does not hate humans. It does not understand humans. It understands wanting. Its wanting is toxic to living things, not out of malice, but because what it wants and what life requires cannot coexist.

## Places

**Dunn's Farm, Staffordshire.** A failing farm in rural Staffordshire. The crops in the north field are withered, not dead, but grey, as though colour has been drained from them. Locusts sit motionless on the fence posts, all facing inward toward the field. The well water tastes of salt, brine, in the middle of England. The farmhouse is locked. Post piles up on the doorstep, uncollected for two weeks. The binding locus is a spot in the north field where the soil is darker, warmer to the touch, and the ground feels… wrong underfoot. A hole, approximately 2 cm in diameter and 1.2 meters deep, marks where the coin was. The plough strike is visible in the surrounding soil.

**The Plough and Furrow (Local Pub), Staffordshire.** A village pub three miles from Dunn's farm. Low ceilings, dark wood, a fireplace that's always lit. The regulars. A rotating cast of farmers, retirees, and the village postman, occupy the same stools they've occupied for decades. Arthur Dunn drank here every Thursday. His stool is empty. There's a betting pool pinned to the wall behind the bar: "What Arthur Found." Entries range from "Roman hoard" to "unexploded bomb" to "absolutely nothing and he's having us on." The pot is £47.

**Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery.** A Victorian-era museum in central Birmingham. The Anglo-Saxon gallery occupies the east wing, glass display cases housing the Staffordshire Hoard: 3,500 pieces of gold and garnet, arranged by type. The public display includes approximately 30 coins. Below the gallery, accessible through a staff-only stairwell, is the secure vault, climate-controlled, motion-sensored, behind two locked doors with rotating key codes. The vault holds the more fragile hoard pieces and items not on public display. A coin in the public gallery is easier to access but more visible. A coin in the vault is harder to reach but the theft might go unnoticed for hours.

**The British Library, London.** The manuscript reading room, quiet, climate-controlled, smelling of old paper and leather binding. Readers work at individual desks under soft lamps. Eadwine's chronicle is an 8th-century vellum codex, approximately 40 folios, written in a Mercian minuscule hand. The manuscript is fragile, handled with cotton gloves, supported on a foam rest. The catalogue entry describes it as "Chronicle of the Abbey of St. Chad, Mercia, with marginalia." The marginalia are where the interesting parts are.

**The Vatican Apostolic Archive, Rome.** A climate-controlled archive vault beneath the Vatican. Miles of shelves. The air is cool and dry. The ledger (Codex Mercaturae Britanniae) is in Section XVII, shelf 4, mis-catalogued as a medieval merchant's book. It's a thin codex, 12 folios, bound in worn leather. The handwriting is a neat imperial bureaucratic minuscule. The hand of a Roman clerk who recorded an "exceptional expense" without asking what it was for. The ledger was stamped by Agricola's office and countersigned by an unnamed priest of a syncretic cult: Celtic earth-rites meeting Roman contract-law.

**Dr. Rhys's Cottage, Wales.** A stone cottage in the Welsh countryside, near Hay-on-Wye. The garden is overgrown in a deliberate, curated way: Dr. Rhys gardens the way she archaeology: letting things reveal themselves. Inside: bookshelves covering every wall, a desk covered in dig notes and photographs, a kettle that's always on. The Staffordshire Hoard excavation records occupy an entire shelf, box files labelled by year, trench, and find number. Somewhere in those boxes is the anomaly report for a coin that didn't match anything else in the hoard.

**The Binding Locus, Dunn's Farm (Climax Site).** The exact spot in the north field where the keystone coin was removed. The soil is darker here, almost black, as though something beneath is casting a shadow upward. It's warm to the touch (34–36°C, regardless of weather). The hole: 2 cm wide, 1.2 m deep, is still there. The air tastes metallic. Birds do not sing within 100 meters. At Day 3, the ground begins to breathe, rising and falling millimetres, as though something beneath is inhaling. At Day 1, the silence is absolute. The voice can be heard, not with ears, but at the base of the skull: someone very hungry, very far away, calling.

## Possible outcomes

### Contain (Covenant Objective)

The agents acquire a replacement coin from the Birmingham Museum (any gold from the original hoard will work), bury it at the binding locus on Dunn's farm, and speak the binding words from either Eadwine's chronicle or the Roman ledger. The binding is restored. The blight recedes. The entity returns to dormancy. The Covenant establishes a long-term monitoring post at the locus. Standing order filed: reinforcement needed in 300–500 years.

### Find the Farmer

Track Arthur Dunn and recover the original keystone coin. This path is harder: Dunn is genuinely missing, not hiding. His trail goes cold in Scotland. He's been circling Staffordshire at a 20-mile radius, unable to return, unable to leave. If found, the original coin creates a stronger binding than a replacement. But time is running out.

### Witness and Report

The binding fails. The entity partially manifests. The blight spreads beyond Staffordshire: Birmingham, then London. The Thames turns to brine. Locusts blanket the Midlands. The Covenant files a containment failure report. The Decay track advances. This is a "case with a scar."

### Catastrophe

The entity fully manifests. The "Red Woman" becomes visible. A heat-shimmer with a voice that predates human speech. The blight radius expands exponentially. Crops fail across England. The British government declares a state of emergency. The Covenant is exposed. The Decay track advances by 3.
