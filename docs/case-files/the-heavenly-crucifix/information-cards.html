I1Supporting Intel

The Replacement

The crucifix on display at Prague Castle is a forgery — exceptional quality, correct weight, correct patina. But the iron is a modern alloy. Someone with metallurgical expertise and access to the original made a copy. The craftsmanship points to a Central European metalsmith with antiquities restoration experience — likely Austrian or southern German. The forgery was swapped within the last six weeks.

When It Happened

The theft occurred on a Monday evening, approximately six weeks ago — the one night the museum closes early and the docents leave at 4 PM. The thief or thieves entered through a service door with a key. No forced entry. No alarms triggered. The forgery was placed in the display case the same night. The museum staff did not notice the substitution.

Not of This Earth

A letter from the Countess's great-grandfather, dated 1878, describing metallurgical tests he commissioned on the crucifix: "The iron does not match any known source in Bohemia. He returned the crucifix and would not accept payment. He died three months later in a foundry accident."

More Iron Beneath the Stones

Excavation at the shrine site reveals additional fragments of meteoric iron beneath the foundation stones. The fragments are unworked — raw ore, dark with a high nickel content, cold to the touch. A metal detector returns strong signals across the clearing. The meteorite was not a single piece. It shattered on impact, scattering fragments across a roughly circular area 30 meters in diameter. The shrine was built to mark — and possibly to contain — the impact site. Each fragment, if forged and worn, would carry the same property as the crucifix.

The Shrine Builders

Among the shrine stones, agents find fragments of Slavic pagan ritual objects — a small iron amulet depicting Perun's axe, pottery shards with solar symbols, a fire pit lined with stones arranged in a radial pattern pointing outward from the impact center. Carbon dating of charcoal from the fire pit places the shrine's construction at approximately 900–950 CE. The builders were a Western Slavic community — likely the Dulebes or Lutici — who witnessed the meteorite impact and incorporated it into their religious practice. The shrine was active for approximately 150 years before Christianization reached the region.

I21Supporting Intel

Why Him

Warhol told an interviewer in 1985: "I don't believe in death. I don't believe in it because you're not around to know you've had it." On June 3, 1968, Valerie Solanas shot him three times. The first bullet passed through his left lung, his spleen, his stomach, his liver, and his right lung. The second entered his right abdomen. The third was stopped by his spine. He was declared clinically dead at 4:51 PM. Surgeons opened his chest and massaged his heart until it restarted. He spent two months in the hospital. He wore a surgical corset for the rest of his life. He never stopped waiting for her to come back. The crucifix is not a purchase. It is an end to an eighteen-year panic attack.

I22Containment Truth — Synthesis

What This Has Always Been About

The Crucifix of St. Vitus is not a Christian relic. It is forged from meteoric iron that fell on the Bohemian Forest in approximately 900 CE. The pagan community that witnessed the impact built a shrine and venerated the iron as a fragment of their sky-god's armor. When the Church arrived, the shrine was destroyed, the iron was reforged into a crucifix, and the origin was suppressed — a secret maintained by the Martinice family and the Vatican's own archives for a thousand years. The iron grants immunity to bodily harm because it is not from Earth. There is more iron at the shrine site. The artifact is replicable. If the Church reclaims it, they face a theological crisis. If the Soviets find the shrine, they gain a strategic material. If Warhol buys it, he wears it until it kills him. The Covenant's mandate is clear: contain the crucifix, seal the shrine, and let no one — not the Church, not the Compact, not the KGB, not a frightened artist — turn this into something worse.
