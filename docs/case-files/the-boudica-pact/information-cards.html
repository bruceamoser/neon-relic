The Hole

In the north field of Dunn's farm, a narrow hole — approximately 2 cm in diameter, 1.2 meters deep — pierces the soil. The edges are fresh, consistent with a plough strike within the last 2–3 weeks. Soil analysis reveals gold residue lining the hole walls and trace elements consistent with 1st-century CE Roman smelting techniques underneath 7th-century Anglo-Saxon metallurgy. Something was buried here in two phases — Roman gold first, Anglo-Saxon gold on top. The Anglo-Saxon layer included a single coin, now missing. The plough pulled it up. The hole is warmer than the surrounding soil (34°C vs. ambient 12°C).

The Staffordshire Hoard

The Staffordshire Hoard — 3,500 pieces of Anglo-Saxon gold and garnet, discovered in 1984 in a field less than 2 miles from Dunn's farm — is held at the Birmingham Museum and Art Gallery. The hoard is the largest collection of Anglo-Saxon gold ever found. Six of seven "key coins" — coins with unusual iconography, identified as a distinct sub-group within the hoard — were recovered and catalogued. The seventh, designated "Unrecovered Item #7" in the dig records, was documented as "likely displaced below excavation depth." The excavation reached 1.0 meter. The coin was at 1.2 meters. The archaeologists missed it by 20 centimetres.

The Red Woman

Local folklore in the Staffordshire villages speaks of "the Red Woman" — a figure in a red dress seen at the edge of fields before bad harvests, before livestock deaths, before things go wrong. The story predates the village — versions appear in 17th-century parish records, in a 12th-century boundary charter, and in Eadwine's 8th-century chronicle under a different name: "the queen who would not leave." Mrs. Albright, a pub regular, has been dreaming of a woman in red standing at the edge of her property for two weeks. "She doesn't move. She just watches. And I wake up feeling like I've lost something I can't name."

Eadwine's Chronicle

An 8th-century vellum codex in the British Library, catalogued as "Chronicle of the Abbey of St. Chad, Mercia." The monk Eadwine records, in a Mercian minuscule hand, the reinforcement of a supernatural binding in the Staffordshire countryside. He describes "the iron-men" (Romans) who "made a bargain with the queen who would not leave, placing gold in the earth that she could not consume." The binding held for 500 years. By Eadwine's time, the gold was "growing thin" — not corroding, but losing its hold. The Mercian king authorized additional gold to be buried — the Staffordshire Hoard. Eadwine was present. He recorded the ritual. He also recorded complaints about the food, the weather, and his abbot.

The Roman Ledger

Codex Mercaturae Britanniae — a thin codex of 12 folios, bound in worn leather, held in Section XVII of the Vatican Apostolic Archive. The handwriting is imperial bureaucratic minuscule — neat, precise, utterly without affect. The entry for the binding reads: "Item: one binding, site CXVII, province of Britannia. Authorized by governor Gnaeus Julius Agricola, third year of his office. Payment: gold, measured by weight. Condition: gold in earth, binding holds. Gold removed, subject returns. Gold returned, binding restored. Note: quantity not specified — a single aureus deemed sufficient per consultation with [name redacted], priest of the [syncretic] cult. Expense classified as exceptional. No further disbursement anticipated." The Romans filed a supernatural pact as an expense report.

I13Supporting Intel

The Terms

The binding's terms, as recorded in the Roman ledger and corroborated by Eadwine's chronicle, are elegantly simple: gold placed in the earth at the binding locus sustains a pact that keeps the entity dormant. Gold removed weakens the binding. Gold returned restores it. The pact specifies "gold, measured by weight" — no quantity, no specific coin, no ritual beyond placement and words. The Romans were precise about loopholes. A single coin, any coin from the original Roman or Anglo-Saxon deposits, is sufficient. The words must be spoken — in Latin or Old English, either works. The entity does not care about language. It cares about the assertion: the debt is paid, the gold is placed, the binding holds.

Eadwine's Marginalia

Between complaints about the abbey's food ("the pottage is thinner than the abbot's mercy") and observations about the weather ("rain without end, as though the sky itself weeps for our sins, which are numerous"), Eadwine recorded the binding words in Old English, with a Latin gloss in the margin. By the weight of the gold and the word of the king, let the earth keep what the earth keeps. Let the sleeper sleep." The Latin version in the Roman ledger is terser: "Aurum in terra. Pactum tenet. Quod ablatum est, redditur. Quod auferre vult, negatur." ("Gold in earth. The pact holds. What was taken is returned. What would take is denied.") Either version works. The entity does not care about language.

I21Supporting Intel

The Ledger's Provenance

The Roman ledger — Codex Mercaturae Britanniae — bears the official stamp of Gnaeus Julius Agricola's office, dated 78 CE. It is countersigned by an unnamed priest described only as "priest of the [syncretic] cult" — the word "syncretic" is in the original Latin. The priest's name has been deliberately scratched from the document — not torn, not erased, but carefully removed with a blade, leaving a rectangular hole in the vellum. Someone wanted this priest forgotten. Father Matteo notes that the handwriting of the removal is 4th-century — Christian, after the Empire's conversion. "The early Church was systematic about erasing pagan practitioners from official records. But they kept the ledger. They knew it was important. They just didn't want anyone to know who wrote it." The pact was a hybrid — Celtic earth-rites combined with Roman contract-law. A negotiation between two systems of understanding the world, designed to contain something neither could destroy.

I22Containment Truth — Synthesis

What This Has Always Been About

After Boudica's defeat in 61 CE, her dying curse — her rage at what was taken from her — was adopted by something older than the Celts: a presence in the earth that had no name, no form, no voice until she gave it one. The Romans couldn't destroy it. They bargained: gold in the earth, the curse sleeps. The Anglo-Saxons reinforced the bargain 500 years later with the Staffordshire Hoard. A monk named Eadwine recorded it between complaints about the food. A farmer named Arthur Dunn broke it by accident in 1987. The entity is not evil. It is incompatible. It wants the land back — not to rule, but to return to a state before human presence. The binding holds as long as gold stays in the earth. The agents must put it back. A single coin. The right words. The right place. The door closes. The sleeper sleeps. For now.
