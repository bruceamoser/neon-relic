I1Supporting Intel

The Town of Cormsil

Cormsil is a farming town of approximately 400 people in the Yorkshire Dales. The earliest recorded settlement is Saxon, but the area shows evidence of pre-Saxon occupation — standing stones, burial mounds, and field patterns that predate the Norman church. The town's name may derive from Old English "Corm" (a personal name) + "syll" (a bog or marsh), though local tradition holds it's older — "the place where something sleeps." The pub is called The Dōmere Arms — no one knows why; it's always been called that. The town is insular, agricultural, and deeply resistant to outside attention.

I2Supporting Intel

The Library Fire

Three weeks ago, on a Thursday evening, the Cormsil Public Library caught fire. The librarian, Eleanor Vane, had been working late cataloguing donations. She fell asleep at her desk; a scented candle ignited a stack of books. The fire spread rapidly through the dry paper and wooden shelving. Twenty-five people were inside — a reading group, a few students, some elderly residents attending a local history talk. All were initially reported dead. But witnesses saw them running from the building into the woods behind the library. They emerged the next morning, unharmed, claiming no memory of the night. The official fire report notes this as "unexplained survivor behavior" and closes with a recommendation for further investigation — which never came.

The Compact of 1882

Among the wreckage of the burned library, a shattered glass case is visible in the reading room rubble. Its brass label, still legible, reads: "Cormsil Compact, 1882 — On Permanent Loan from the Town Council." The case is empty. Ash fragments within suggest the document was inside when the fire reached it. No one in town can say what the compact was — most didn't know it existed. The older residents recall that there used to be an annual "reading day" at the church, discontinued around the time of the Great War, but no one remembers what was read or why.

Eleanor's Testimony

Eleanor Vane tells her story in a voice worn thin by sleeplessness. She fell asleep at her desk — she'd been working late, she was tired, the candle was scented with lavender, it was supposed to be calming. She woke to smoke. She tried to warn people. She saw them running — not toward the exits, but toward the back, toward the woods. She doesn't remember how she got out. She remembers the sound of the roof collapsing. Since the fire, every day, the 25 survivors visit her — one by one, sometimes in pairs now — to tell her they forgive her. "They don't want me to die," she says. "They want me to know. And I do. I know. I've known since the first one walked through my door. But knowing doesn't stop them coming." She has not slept in 19 days.

The document is signed by "Arthur Hailes, Vicar" and a second name that is deliberately obscured — the ink has been scratched away. The Fire Investigator's Report

The official report from the North Yorkshire Fire Service confirms: the fire started from a scented candle on the librarian's desk, ignited a stack of donated books awaiting cataloguing, and spread through the building's dry timber construction. Cause: accidental. The report includes an unusual section titled "Survivor Behavior": "All 25 casualties were observed exiting the building through the rear fire door and proceeding into the woodland behind the library. This behavior is inconsistent with standard fire evacuation patterns, which would direct occupants toward the front entrance and the village green. When located the following morning at approximately 0700 hours, all 25 were at the treeline, disoriented, with no memory of the intervening 9 hours. Medical examination found no injuries. This investigator recommends further inquiry, which was not pursued due to resource constraints."

The Society's Manual

Behind a loose stone in the crypt of St. Cuthbert's Church, agents find a small, leather-bound volume — a handwritten field guide, Victorian in origin, approximately 80 pages. The title page reads: "A Practical Guide to Negotiation with Subterranean Presences — For Field Use Only — The Society." The manual is unsettlingly knowledgeable. It describes entities "bound to specific geographies, predating human settlement, responsive to moral imbalance — specifically, death by neglect, violence, or preventable error." It outlines methods for acknowledgment rituals, negotiation frameworks, and binding ceremonies. A section titled "The Yorkshire Presence" matches the Dōmere exactly: "Called Dōmere by the Saxons — the Doomer. Cannot be slain. Responds to compact. Prefers acknowledgment to confrontation. Do not attempt exorcism — it predates the faith and does not recognize its authority." The manual references "Colne's manuscript (1642, British Library)" and "the standing stone at Cormsil — the original site, pre-Saxon."

Colne's Manuscript — The Dōmere

Sir Geoffrey Colne's 1642 manuscript, "Of Certain Subterranean Presences in the County of York," identifies the entity beneath Cormsil. Key passages: "The presence is called Dōmere by the Saxons — the Doomer, the Judge. It predates the Celts, the Brigantes, and all known settlement. The Romans record an attempt to destroy it during the conquest of the Brigantes (circa 70 CE); the attempt failed. The presence cannot be killed — it is not alive in any sense we understand. It is a function of the earth, as gravity is a function of mass. It responds to wrongful death. It cannot abide an unbalanced moral ledger. The Saxons built a church above its resting place, believing confinement would weaken it. They were incorrect — the presence was merely... waiting." Colne describes his own failed attempt to exorcise it in 1642, noting: "I treated it as a demon. It is not a demon. It predates the faith and does not recognize its authority. It ignored my ritual entirely."

The Vicar's Letter

Vicar Arthur Hailes's sealed letter to the Bishop of Ripon, dated March 1883. The letter is cautious, uncomfortable, and carefully worded: "My Lord Bishop, I write concerning events in this parish which I cannot fully explain and which I am not certain I should attempt to. A compact has been reached between the town and a presence I am reluctant to name. The negotiations were mediated by persons who identified themselves only as The Society. I found them unorthodox but effective. They provided a volume of reference which I was instructed to return upon completion of the compact. I have not done so. I have hidden this volume in the church, for I believe the knowledge it contains is too dangerous to be lost to us and too valuable to be surrendered to those who would suppress it. The Society, I suspect, would understand. They have no name worth recording, for they prefer to remain unremembered. I ask your guidance, or your silence — whichever your conscience permits."

The Failed Binding of 1642

Sir Geoffrey Colne's manuscript describes his attempt to bind the Dōmere in 1642. He approached it as a demon to be exorcised — Latin rites, holy water, the authority of the Church. The spirit ignored him entirely. "It did not resist," Colne writes. "It did not flee. It simply... failed to notice. It was as though I had attempted to command the tide to stop, or the wind to change direction. The presence is not hostile to our faith — it is indifferent. It was here before Bethlehem and will be here after Rome is dust." Colne learned from his failure. His manuscript becomes, in its later sections, a corrected field report: what the spirit actually responds to (acknowledgment, not command), what it wants (recognition of wrongs), and what might work instead of exorcism (negotiation, compact, binding through consent rather than force).

The Riddle

In the margins of Colne's manuscript, written in a careful 17th-century hand, is an Old English riddle:

"I was here before the first king knelt.
I weigh what no scale can hold.
I sleep when remembered, wake when forgot.
The Saxons named me. Speak my name —
and I will answer."

The answer is "Dōmere" — the Doomer, the Judge. The riddle encodes the spirit's true name without writing it plainly. Colne lived in an age of witch-hunters; he knew some knowledge was dangerous to record openly. The name, when spoken at the standing stone, forces the spirit to acknowledge the speaker. It is the first component of the silencing ritual.

The volunteer must understand what they're choosing. Margaret Hale (NPC5) is the intended volunteer.

I22Containment Truth — Synthesis

What This Has Always Been About

The Dōmere is older than humanity. It was in the earth before the first Celts arrived, before the Brigantes dug their well, before the Saxons built their church, before the Normans raised St. Cuthbert's stone by stone. It is not a ghost. It is not a demon. It is not a god. It is a function — justice without mercy, consequence without choice, a ledger that must balance. It does not hate. It does not love. It responds to wrongful death the way water responds to gravity — inevitably, impersonally, and without end.

The compact of 1882 was the first time anyone treated it as a party to a negotiation rather than a monster to be slain. The Society understood what Colne learned: the Dōmere responds to acknowledgment. The annual reading kept it asleep for 105 years — not because the ritual had power, but because the acknowledgment satisfied its function. Someone remembered. Someone said the names of the wronged. That was enough.

The library fire destroyed the compact. The spirit woke in a world where a librarian fell asleep at her desk and 25 people burned. It found negligence. It found preventable death. It found no acknowledgment. It is furious — not with malice, but with the absolute fury of a law that has been broken and forgotten. It wants to expand because it sees a world full of preventable deaths and no one answering for them. It wants to fill the world with consequences.

The agents can silence it, or negotiate, or fail and watch it spread. The choice is theirs. But the Dōmere will not be the same as it was in 1882. It has been forgotten once. It will not trust again easily. Whatever terms it accepts will be harsher. Whatever binding is placed will be tested. The question is not whether the agents can defeat the Dōmere — they cannot. The question is whether they can convince something older than humanity that the world is still worth sparing.
